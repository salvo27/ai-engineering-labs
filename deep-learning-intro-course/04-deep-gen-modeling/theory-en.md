# Deep Generative Modeling

In this section we will look at the basics of generative modeling.

We will see how to create neural networks that do not just learn patterns from data, but that are able to understand how to model the distributions of the data, so that they can replicate these distributions to create new instances of data.

To see how powerful these algorithms are, we will look at an example of AI generation. Try to guess which of these images are not generated by AI:

![Quiz](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/quiz.png)

In this case, the real image is C. A and B were generated using Nano Banana 2 from Gemini.

## Supervised training vs unsupervised training

Up until now, up to this chapter, we have always dealt with neural networks where training was supervised (Feed Forward, RNN, CNN). This means that given an input, the real output, the label, was also given.
The goal has always been to learn a functional mapping between x (input) and y (label, output): for classification, regression, object detection, semantic segmentation, and so on.

But in Deep Learning there is a much larger class of problems, namely unsupervised training. Here we give our network only the input data, without any label, without any real output. The goal is to understand, to find, a model that captures these data distributions, so as to learn the main structure of this data, namely the distribution itself.
For example: clustering, dimensionality reduction.

So how does **generative modeling** work?
It takes as input examples from a distribution and learns a neural network model that represents this probability distribution.

This is the main and essential concept of generative AI: capturing and learning probability distributions from data.

We can use this concept in a couple of ways:
- density estimation: given data that follows a distribution, we learn a model of this distribution, that is, we describe the probabilistic model according to which this data is distributed;
- if we have actually learned a probability distribution, at this point we can generate using this distribution, this model.

But how do we learn these distributions starting from the data?

We learn the most important features of a dataset and use them to build a more accurate and robust representation of the real distribution.
This also allows us to do what is called debiasing.

We know in fact that in many real cases, the datasets that are collected contain bias, that is, systematic distortions in the data, often due to the way they were collected.

For example, a dataset of faces used for facial recognition might contain many more images of people of a certain ethnicity than others, or a medical dataset might be made up mostly of male patients.

![Bias](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/bias.png)

Models trained on these datasets end up capturing and replicating the same biases, producing results that are less accurate or even discriminatory for underrepresented groups.

Using generative modeling techniques, we can make the model learn the underlying distribution in a more balanced way, generating synthetic data that corrects or balances the distortions present in the original dataset.

Another important example is **outlier** detection.
Let's remember again that generative models capture these probability distributions from the data. In cases such as autonomous driving, or autopilot for an airplane, or in medicine, there can be edge cases, cases that happen very rarely (for example, an animal suddenly appearing on the road, thick fog, and so on). How do we capture these cases? How do we use generative models to capture these potentially problematic events and correct the behavior?

### Why generative models?

This is exactly the core of generative models: once the probability distribution of the data has been learned, we can sample from it, that is, draw new instances that follow that same distribution, as if they were real data.
This mechanism is the foundation of all modern Generative AI, and it applies to very different domains:

- **natural language**: models like ChatGPT generate text by sampling from distributions learned over billions of words. The result is coherent answers, translations, summaries, code, and so on;

- **images and video**;

- **molecules**: in the pharmaceutical and biological field, generative models are used to design new molecular structures, greatly accelerating research on drugs and proteins;

In all of these cases the principle is the same: the model does not memorize the data, but captures its deep structure, and from that structure it is able to generate something new.

## Latent variable models

There are mainly two classic families of generative models:

- **VAE** (Variational Autoencoders): these were the dominant paradigm until a few years ago, and they remain fundamental for understanding the principles behind generative AI;

- **GAN** (Generative Adversarial Networks): these dominated image generation for several years, producing visually very convincing results.

More recently a third family has taken over: Diffusion Models, which today form the basis of systems such as Stable Diffusion and DALL-E.

The most important question, however, is:

**what do we mean by the "most relevant features" of the data, and what does latent mean?**

### The myth of the cave

To answer this question, let's use one of the most famous philosophical metaphors: Plato's myth of the cave.

![Myth](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/myth.jpg)

Imagine some prisoners chained inside a cave, with their backs to the wall.
Behind them a fire is burning, and between the fire and the prisoners some figures pass by.
The prisoners only see the shadows projected on the wall in front of them, they never see the real objects.
For the prisoners, those shadows are reality.
But we know that the shadows are only a reduced representation of something richer and more complex that exists elsewhere.

In generative modeling it works in a similar way: the data we observe (images, text, sounds) are like the shadows on the wall.
They are the visible manifestation of deeper, more abstract structures that we do not observe directly.
These hidden structures are the latent variables.
The latent space is therefore the room behind the cave: a compressed, abstract world where the essential features of the data live.
The generative model learns to move within this space, and by sampling from it, projects new shadows onto the wall, that is, it generates new data.

By modeling these latent variables and using the encoding approach, the goal is similar to this myth of the cave. The data is represented by a distribution, that is, what we observe, but underneath it has other characteristics.
With latent variable modeling our goal is to capture this behavior, these hidden characteristics, and to move within this space.

## Autoencoders

The dominant approach for doing this is the encoding approach, and it started with the **Autoencoder** model.

This is a very elegant approach, based on the idea of self-encoding the inputs of the neural network.
The principle is to take raw data as input, and train our neural network, through several layers, to arrive at a representation of the same initial input but in a leaner form, that is, a representation of the most important features of this data.
The idea is to learn a mapping that takes us from the initial data distribution to this lower dimensional space z.

![Autoencoders](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/autoencoders.png)

In a sense, this is a kind of compression of the information in our data. Our data might be very complex, but by reducing its dimensionality, we can move around its most important characteristics.

But what is our goal? Why do we want to do this compression work?
The idea is very clever: we have the input data, we have compressed this distribution, and starting from this compressed model, we want to try to regenerate the initial data.

This is the idea behind autoencoders: starting from this dimension of raw data, building this dimension of compressed, essential data, and going back from this encoding to the input space.

![How autoencoders work](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/autoencoders_work.png)

At this point, our goal is to minimize this loss function.
The loss function simply looks, for example in the case of images, at the pixel by pixel difference between the input and the reconstruction of the input.
There is no dependence on labels, only the input is used.

This is a very powerful idea, because we start introducing the compression of very complex data, large inputs, into something of smaller dimension, the latent space z.

We do not want this compressed dimension to be too large, because that is not our goal, and we do not want it to be too small either, because compressing it still defines a kind of bottleneck.

The main idea is that we have this bottleneck layer, this set of latent variables z, which forces the training of a compressed representation. We can train the model in an unsupervised way, by reconstructing this input, computing the loss, and then minimizing it.
This is exactly the self encoding idea used by autoencoders.

In practice this approach is very elegant, but it is not very useful for generating new samples, because this approach is deterministic. That is, once we have trained the autoencoder, if we take input x and pass it through the same model again, the output will be exactly the same every time.

So we can say that it is not very useful for generating samples, because there is no concept of variability in the samples we generate, nor of a probability distribution.

Another idea that was proposed to address this problem is that of VAEs, namely Variational AutoEncoders.

## VAEs (Variational AutoEncoders)

Here the main idea is based on the following question: can we introduce a kind of probabilistic twist into our idea of self encoding?

With standard autoencoders, what we produce is deterministic. The model is trained, and if we always give it the same input, we always get the same output.

In VAEs, instead of having a z that produces deterministic outputs, we can replace it with a particular sampling operation. Instead of learning the latent variables directly, we learn a mean and a variance for each latent variable.
The idea is that this mean and variance can parameterize the probability distribution of the latent variables, allowing us to sample from these variables and produce new outputs.

This means that we move from learning a set of variables z, to learning a vector of means and standard deviations for each of these latent variables.

![VAEs](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/vaes.png)

Let's formalize this concept, and think about our set of weights. Instead of a deterministic mapping, the encoder, given the input x, now captures a probability distribution over the latent variables.
The decoder, on the other hand, is computing a probability distribution over our space x, given our latent variables z.

We parameterize this with weights, phi and theta, which define these encoders and decoders respectively.
These weights are exactly what is learned by our neural network.

But how do we do this?
Let's look at how we build the loss in VAEs.

$$ \mathcal{L}(\phi, \theta, x) = \text{reconstruction loss} + \text{regularization term} $$

The reconstruction loss is the loss we have always seen up to now.
What about this regularization term, then? It is a kind of loss on the distribution, which serves precisely to reinforce the learning of a good probability distribution.

The most important thing is to understand that this loss depends on these two sets of weights, phi and theta, and that during the learning and training of the neural network, we will try to optimize this loss with respect to these two sets of weights, those of the encoder and those of the decoder. These two terms dictate the function on which we apply gradient descent.

Let's analyze the regularization term of our loss.
Let's remember that our goal is to learn the distribution of the latent variables, parameterized by mu and sigma.
As in the case of the reconstruction loss, we compute a kind of distance, but in this case it is a distance between distributions.

The encoder is inferring (that is, deducing, estimating something starting from observed data) this distribution of the latent variables $q_{\phi}(z|x)$.

$$ D_{KL}(q_{\phi}(z|x) \parallel p(z)) $$

This term measures how much the distribution learned by the encoder $q_{\phi}(z|x)$ deviates from a reference distribution $p(z)$, which we choose to be a standard Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

In practice, we are telling the network: "learn a distribution over the latent space, but make sure it does not stray too far from a simple, regular Gaussian."
This has two effects, which serve to satisfy the requirements of a good latent space:

- it **regularizes** the latent space, preventing the model from collapsing into degenerate solutions;
- it **ensures** that by sampling from the standard Gaussian, the decoder produces meaningful outputs, that is, that the latent space is continuous and navigable.

In practice, in VAEs, no matter how large the encoder or decoder is, the standard Gaussian is almost always used as the prior.
The term D can be seen as a kind of divergence between the learned distribution and the standard Gaussian.

Why do we use this specific distribution? To understand this, we always need to keep in mind another question, namely the intuition behind why we want to learn these latent variables, why we want to navigate this latent space, and what characteristics and properties this space of latent variables must have.

![Priors on the latent distribution](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/priors_latent.png)

So, what are the characteristics that a latent space must have:

- **continuity**: points that are close together in the latent space must have a similar meaning after decoding;
- **completeness**: there must be a kind of semantic structure that is captured and learned by this latent space, such that when we sample from the latent space and decode, the output is something semantically sensible and meaningful.

Without any regularization, the problem is that we would lose these two desirable characteristics. We could have points that are close together in the latent space, but that, after decoding, would not have a similar meaning, nor even a meaningful one.

So why does the normal distribution specifically give us these characteristics?
Does any distribution guarantee us these properties, or does the normal distribution have something special that makes us prefer it?

Without regularization, the latent space can degenerate in two ways.

To understand this, let's imagine training a VAE on images of human faces: natural groups, called clusters, will tend to form in the latent space, one for each main characteristic. For example, a cluster for smiling faces, one for faces with glasses, one for faces with dark hair, and so on.

The problem is that without regularization these clusters can degenerate in two ways:

- **small variances**: each cluster becomes an isolated dot instead of a cloud, that is, a region of space with a certain extent, in which nearby points represent similar variations of the same concept. The encoder maps each face to a precise, separate point. If we sampled a point just next to it, the decoder would not know what to produce, generating a distorted, meaningless image;

- **distant means**: the clusters end up in far apart regions of the latent space, with large gaps between them. By sampling a point in one of these gaps, the decoder would generate something undefined, neither a smiling face nor one with glasses, but a corrupted, incomprehensible image.

The result is a latent space that is fragmented and not navigable.

By using a standard Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ as the prior instead, we get exactly the opposite: the means are pushed toward the origin and the variances are regularized toward 1. This guarantees two fundamental properties:

- **continuity**: the regularized variances force the clusters to expand and overlap at their edges. Moving gradually through the latent space between the region of smiling faces and the region of faces with glasses, we will see smooth, natural transitions between the two characteristics, generating faces that combine both;

- **completeness**: the means centered around the origin push the clusters closer to each other, eliminating the gaps. In this way, every point in the latent space produces a realistic, meaningful face when passed to the decoder, without dead zones that generate corrupted images.

![Normal prior](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/regularization_normal.png)

Does this mean that all the latent variables will end up between 0 and 1? No, because in the loss we also have the other term, the reconstruction term.

So what we need to do is to properly balance these reconstruction and regularization losses.

The last piece of this puzzle is backpropagation.
We said that instead of learning z directly, our encoder learns mu and sigma, and samples z from these. But here a fundamental problem arises.

Backpropagation works because every operation in the network is differentiable, that is, the gradient can flow backward through all the layers and update the weights. But sampling from a distribution is a stochastic, random operation, and it is not differentiable. We cannot let the gradient flow through a random operation.

How do we solve this problem?

With a very elegant trick, called the **reparametrization trick**.

Instead of sampling z directly from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, we separate the random part from the part that the network needs to learn:

$$z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

The idea is simple: we sample epsilon from a fixed standard Gaussian, which does not depend on the network's weights, and then we use mu and sigma to transform this sample into the z we want.

In this way the randomness is isolated in epsilon, which is a fixed external input, while mu and sigma remain differentiable operations through which the gradient can flow normally.

The result is that we can train the VAE end to end with backpropagation, learning at the same time to build a well structured latent space and to reconstruct the original inputs.

![Reparametrizing](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/reparametrizing.png)

This diagram makes the problem and the solution visual.

In the **original form** (on the left), z is a stochastic node (orange circle): it is sampled directly from the distribution $q_{\phi}(z|x)$, which depends on the weights phi and on the input x. The problem is that the gradient, in order to propagate backward from f to phi, would have to pass through this stochastic node, but it cannot: there is no way to differentiate a random sampling operation with respect to its parameters.

In the **reparametrized form** (on the right), we move the randomness into a separate node: epsilon, sampled from a standard Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ that does not depend on phi or on x. At this point, z becomes a deterministic node (blue diamond), computed as a function $z = g(\phi, x, \epsilon)$.

The result is that now the gradient can flow freely from f, through z, all the way to phi and x, because the entire path is made up of deterministic, differentiable operations. The random part, epsilon, is isolated at the edges of the graph and no longer blocks backpropagation.

The cool thing about VAEs is that since we are learning this latent space, we get some degree of interpretability or control when we sample. In the case of well trained, good quality VAEs, we can think of these latent variables as capturing individual features, so that if we perturb the values of the variables in the space, we can get small differences, in this example in the pose of the head:

![Perturbation](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/perturbation.png)

We could therefore combine CNNs and VAEs to debias facial detection systems, and we could use the latent distributions to reach this goal.

### So, let's do a final recap of what we do in VAEs:

**How the model works:**

1. We **compress** the representation of our data into a lower dimensional latent space, capturing the most important features;

2. we **reconstruct** the input starting from this compressed representation, which allows us to train the model in an unsupervised way, without needing labels;

3. we use **reparametrization** to make the end to end path differentiable, allowing backpropagation through the entire model.

**What we can do with a trained VAE:**

4. **interpret** the latent space: by moving along a specific direction (perturbing z), we can observe how the output changes gradually and meaningfully, for example by varying the expression of a face or the rotation angle of an object;

5. **generate** new samples, by sampling directly from the prior $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and passing the result to the decoder.

## GANs (Generative Adversarial Networks)

Up until now we have seen an approach that *explicitly* learns a probability distribution: the encoder estimates mu and sigma, the decoder reconstructs, and the loss tells us how close we are to the true distribution.

But what would happen if, instead of telling the model *how* the distribution should look, we let it discover this on its own, through a challenge?

In 2014 Ian Goodfellow proposed an idea that changed the face of generative AI: **Generative Adversarial Networks**. The idea is as simple as it is brilliant: pit two neural networks against each other, in a zero sum game, and let them improve each other until one of the two, the **generator**, becomes so good at creating data that it is indistinguishable from real data.

They were truly revolutionary. For years, GANs produced the most realistic synthetic images ever seen, giving rise to deepfakes, faces of nonexistent people, AI generated artwork, and an entire family of variants (dozens of different architectures, jokingly known as the "GAN zoo"). Even though today they have been partly superseded by diffusion models, the adversarial principle behind them is still one of the most influential ideas in deep learning.

Let's see how this "game" works.

### The basic idea

Let's look at the problem from a different perspective than with VAEs.

Instead of *explicitly* modeling the probability density of the data (as we did with mu, sigma and the KL divergence), why don't we simply try to **sample** new instances, skipping the problem of describing the distribution altogether?

The problem is that we cannot sample directly from a complex distribution like that of real data, we have no way to describe it in closed form.

The solution is elegant: we sample from something simple, for example random noise, and we learn a **transformation** that takes this noise into the space of real data.

This is exactly the task of the **Generator Network G**: it takes a noise vector z as input, and transforms it into a sample $x_{fake}$, that is, a synthetic instance that should resemble the real data x.

In other words, G learns a function that maps a simple, known space (the noise) into a complex, unknown space (the distribution of real data).

But how do we figure out if G is doing a good job, that is, whether $x_{fake}$ is really similar to x? This is where the second protagonist of GANs comes into play.

![Discriminator](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/discriminator.png)

At this point the second protagonist comes into play: the **Discriminator Network D**.

D's task is simple: look at an image x and say whether it is **real** (coming from the dataset) or **fake** (created by the generator G). In practice, D is a binary classifier, which produces an output y, the probability that the input is real.

The name "adversarial" comes precisely from this dynamic: the two networks have opposite goals, and they are trained together, in competition.

- the **generator** G receives noise z and produces $x_{fake}$, trying to fool D, that is, to produce images that D classifies as real;

- the **discriminator** D receives both $x_{real}$ (real data from the dataset) and $x_{fake}$ (data produced by G), and tries to tell them apart correctly.

It is a genuine zero sum game: every time D gets better at recognizing fakes, G is forced to improve in order to produce more convincing fakes, and vice versa. Over time, this "tug of war" pushes G to produce outputs that are increasingly close to the real data distribution, until they become practically indistinguishable.

![Training GANs](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/training_gans.png)

$$ \arg \max_{D} \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{x}} [ \log D(G(\mathbf{z})) + \log (1 - D(\mathbf{x})) ] $$

Remember that D produces a value between 0 and 1: the closer it is to 1, the more convinced D is that the input is real.

This expression is made up of two terms:

- $\log D(G(\mathbf{z}))$: this concerns the **fake** data, produced by the generator starting from the noise z. The term is maximized when $D(G(z))$ is close to 1, that is, when D classifies the fake data as real;

- $\log (1 - D(\mathbf{x}))$: this concerns the **real** data x. The term is maximized when $D(x)$ is close to 0, that is, when D classifies the real data as fake.

By maximizing the sum of these two terms, D would be trained to do exactly the opposite of its task: to mistake real data for fake and fake data for real.

### The objective

Now that we have seen what D wants, we can complete the picture by adding G:

$$ \arg \min_{G} \max_{D} \; \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}} [\log D(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}} [\log (1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))] $$

This is the complete formulation of the adversarial game, and it captures in a single expression the opposite goals of the two networks:

- **D** tries to **maximize** this expression: it wants to assign a high score $D(x)$ to real data and a low score $D(G(z))$ to fake data, in order to tell them apart correctly;

- **G** tries to **minimize** the same expression: it wants $D(G(z))$ to be high, that is, it wants the discriminator to mistake its fake outputs for real data. To minimize the expression, G therefore has to make $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ as small as possible, pushing $D(G(z))$ toward 1.

During training, we alternate between the two steps: we update D to improve its ability to distinguish real from fake, then we update G to improve its ability to fool D. The two networks chase each other in a dynamic equilibrium, until G becomes so good that D can no longer do better than 50 percent accuracy, that is, it can no longer tell real from fake.

Once trained, we can think of G as a function that **transforms** a simple distribution into a complex one: it takes points from Gaussian noise $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and maps them into the space of real data x.

But what exactly happens in this transformation? What does G "see" when it looks at a point z?

![GANs are distribution transformers](../../assets/deep-learning-intro-course/04/gans_distribution.png)

The image below shows this very effectively: we take two points in the latent space (z1 and z2), and we move gradually from one to the other, generating an image for each intermediate point. The result is a smooth, continuous transition, from a black swan to a robin, passing through plausible intermediate forms.

This is exactly the same continuity principle we saw when talking about VAEs: the latent space is not a collection of isolated points, but a continuous, navigable space, in which moving gradually produces gradual, coherent variations in the generated output.

### CycleGANs: transforming one domain into another

We have seen GANs as transformers: they take points from Gaussian noise and map them into the space of real data (Gaussian noise → target data manifold).

But if we generalize this idea, why limit ourselves to starting from noise? We can use the same principle to transform **one data domain into another**: data manifold X → data manifold Y. This is the idea behind **CycleGANs**.

#### How it works

A CycleGAN learns two transformations at the same time, using **unpaired data**, that is, without needing one to one corresponding examples between the two domains:

- a generator **G** that transforms X into Y;
- a generator **F** that transforms Y into X (the reverse path);
- two discriminators, $D_X$ and $D_Y$, which check whether the generated outputs are indistinguishable from real data in their respective domain.

The name "cycle" comes from the fact that the model is also trained to respect a **cycle consistency**: if we transform an image from X to Y with G, and then bring it back from Y to X with F, we should get (approximately) the original image. This constraint is what allows the model to be trained without needing pairs of corresponding images.

The classic example is the horse to zebra transformation: the model learns to "add stripes" to a horse to make it look like a zebra, and vice versa, without ever having seen a paired horse photo and zebra photo of the same animal in the same pose.

#### Not just images

This principle is not limited to images. The same approach can be applied to any kind of data that can be represented as an image, for example **audio**: a waveform can be converted into a **spectrogram** (a visual representation of frequencies over time), and a CycleGAN can learn to transform the spectrogram of one voice (or instrument) into that of another, and then reconstruct the resulting waveform. This is the principle behind many voice conversion and audio style transfer applications.

### When the two are combined: deepfakes

The two applications we have seen, image transformation and audio transformation, can be combined together. This is exactly the principle behind **deepfakes**: using generative models to simultaneously replace the face and voice of a person in a video with those of another person.

The face is transformed using image to image translation techniques (such as the CycleGANs seen above, or more modern variants), frame by frame, keeping expressions and movements consistent with the original video. The voice, at the same time, is converted using voice conversion techniques on the spectrograms, so as to match the timbre and intonation of the target person.

The result is a synthetic video in which a person appears to say and do things they never said or did, generated entirely by combining these generative techniques.

This is also an opportunity to reflect on an important point: the same technology that we use for debiasing datasets, for artistic generation, or for assistive voice synthesis, can be used to create extremely convincing fake content. Understanding how these models work is the first step toward recognizing their limits and risks, and toward developing tools capable of detecting artificially generated content.

## Summary: two paths to generation

We have seen two families of generative models, two different paths to reach the same goal: generating new data starting from a learned distribution.

**Autoencoders and Variational Autoencoders (VAE)**

They learn a lower dimensional **latent space**, compressing the data into a few essential variables, and then **sample** from this space to generate reconstructions of the input. Their strength is the explicit, interpretable structure of the latent space: we can move around within it and observe continuous, meaningful transitions between outputs.

**Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN)**

They work in a completely different way: two networks, a **generator** and a **discriminator**, compete with each other in an adversarial game. The generator learns to transform noise into realistic data, driven by the attempt to fool the discriminator. Their strength is the quality and realism of the generated outputs, often higher than that of VAEs.

Both approaches share the central idea of this section: capturing the probability distribution of the data in order to generate new instances of it. In the next sections we will see how these principles evolve further in **diffusion models**, the architecture behind today's most advanced image generation systems.
